> [!problem] Problem 1
> Define the group
> $$SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z}) = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \mid a, b, c, d \in \mathbb{Z},\ ad - bc = 1 \right\}.$$
> The group multiplication is matrix multiplication. Consider the two elements
> $$T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad S = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
> Show that the group $SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})$ is generated by $T$ and $S$.

**Proof:**
Let $M = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \in SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$ with $ad-bc=1$.

While $c \neq 0$:  
- If $|a| \ge |c|$, left‑multiply by $T^{\mp k}$ to replace $(a,c)$ by $(a\mp kc,\,c)$ (choose sign to reduce $|a|$).  
- If $|a| < |c|$, left‑multiply by $S$ to swap the rows (up to sign).  
This is exactly the Euclidean algorithm on $(a,c)$, so after finitely many steps we obtain a matrix $\begin{pmatrix} a' & b' \\ c' & d' \end{pmatrix}$ with determinant $1$ and $a'=0$ or $c'=0$. We multiply the matrix by $S$ if $a'=0,c'\neq {0}$, otherwise do nothing.

From $a'd' = 1$ we have $a'=d'=\pm1$.  
If $a'=d'=-1$, left‑multiply by $S^2 = -I$ (which is in $\langle S,T \rangle$) to make $a'=d'=1$.  
Hence the matrix becomes $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & b' \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = T^{b'}$.

Since every step only used $T^{\pm1}$ and $S$, the original $M$ is a word in $T$ and $S$. Therefore $SL_2(\mathbb{Z}) = \langle T, S \rangle$.

> [!problem] Problem 2
> Show that the symmetric group $S_n$ is generated by $(12)$ and $(123\ldots n)$.

**Proof:**
Let $\sigma = (1\;2\;3\;\dots\;n)$ and $\tau = (1\;2)$.

We get all adjacent transpositions $(k\;k+1)$ from $\tau$ and $\sigma$ by conjugation:
$$
\sigma^k \tau \sigma^{-k} = (k+1,\;k+2) \qquad (k=0,1,\dots,n-2).
$$


Every permutation in $S_n$ can be written as a product of transpositions, and every transposition $(i\;j)$ ($i<j$) can be written as a product of adjacent transpositions:
$$
(i\;j) = (i\;i+1)(i+1\;i+2)\cdots(j-1\;j)\cdots(i+1\;i+2)(i\;i+1).
$$
Hence the adjacent transpositions generate $S_n$.

Therefore $S_n = \langle \tau,\;\sigma \rangle$.

> [!problem] Problem 3
> Let $K$ be a normal subgroup of $G$. Formulate and prove the universal property of the quotient group $G/K$ using homomorphism to another group $H$.

**Universal property of the quotient group:**
Let $K\triangleleft G$ be a normal subgroup, $\pi:G\to G/K$ the canonical projection $\pi(g)=gK$.  
If $f:G\to H$ is a group homomorphism with $K\subseteq\ker f$, then there exists a unique homomorphism $\bar f:G/K\to H$ such that $f=\bar f\circ\pi$, i.e. the diagram commutes:

$$
\begin{array}{ccc}
G & \xrightarrow{\;f\;} & H \\
{\scriptstyle\pi}\big\downarrow & \nearrow_{\scriptstyle\bar f} & \\
G/K & &
\end{array}
$$

**Proof:**
**Uniqueness.** For any $gK\in G/K$, $\bar f(gK)=\bar f(\pi(g))=f(g)$. Hence $\bar f$ is uniquely determined by $f$.

**Well‑definedness.** Define $\bar f(gK)=f(g)$. If $gK=g'K$, then $g^{-1}g'\in K\subseteq\ker f$, so $f(g^{-1}g')=1$. Hence $f(g)=f(g')$.

**Homomorphism.** $\bar f(gK\cdot hK)=\bar f(ghK)=f(gh)=f(g)f(h)=\bar f(gK)\bar f(hK)$.

Thus $\bar f$ is the required homomorphism.

> [!problem] Problem 4
> Let $H$ be a subgroup of $G$ with $[G:H]=2$. Prove that $H$ is a normal subgroup of $G$.

**Proof:**
Since $[G:H]=2$, the only left cosets are $H$ and $gH$ for any $g\notin H$, and the only right cosets are $H$ and $Hg$. But the complement of $H$ in $G$ is both $gH$ and $Hg$, so $gH=Hg$ for all $g\in G$; hence $H\trianglelefteq G$.

> [!problem] Problem 5
> For a group $G$, define its commutator subgroup to be the subgroup generated by the set
> $$
> \{ghg^{-1}h^{-1} \in G \mid g, h \in G\}.
>$$
> (i)  Prove that the commutator subgroup is always a normal subgroup.
> (ii) Compute the commutator subgroup of $GL_n(\mathbb{C})$.
> (iii) Compute the commutator subgroup of $S_n$.

**Proof:**
**(i)** Let $[G,G]$ be the commutator subgroup. For any $x\in G$ and a generator $[a,b]=aba^{-1}b^{-1}$, we have
$$
x[a,b]x^{-1}=xaba^{-1}b^{-1}x^{-1}=(xax^{-1})(xbx^{-1})(xax^{-1})^{-1}(xbx^{-1})^{-1}=[xax^{-1},xbx^{-1}]\in[G,G].
$$
Since conjugation by $x$ sends each generator of $[G,G]$ into $[G,G]$, it sends the whole subgroup $[G,G]$ into itself. Hence $[G,G]\trianglelefteq G$.

**(ii)** For any $A, B \in GL_n(\mathbb{C})$, 
  $$
\det([A,B]) = \det(ABA^{-1}B^{-1}) = 1 \Rightarrow [A,B] \in SL_n(\mathbb{C}),
$$
so $[GL_n(\mathbb{C}), GL_n(\mathbb{C})] \subseteq SL_n(\mathbb{C})$.

Conversely, for any elementary matrix $E_{ij}(a)$ (with $i \ne j$, $a \in \mathbb{C}$, let $D = \operatorname{diag}(d_1,\dots,d_n)$ be an invertible diagonal matrix with $d_i \ne d_j$. Then
$$
[E_{ij}(1), D] = E_{ij}(1) D E_{ij}(1)^{-1} D^{-1} = E_{ij}\!\big(1 - \tfrac{d_j}{d_i}\big).
$$
By choosing $d_i, d_j$ suitably, we can obtain $E_{ij}(a)$ for any $a \in \mathbb{C}^\times$; for $a=0$ it is trivial.  

Since $SL_n(\mathbb{C})$ is generated by elementary matrices, and each elementary matrix lies in the commutator subgroup, we get $SL_n(\mathbb{C}) \subseteq [GL_n(\mathbb{C}), GL_n(\mathbb{C})]$.


Thus, $[GL_n(\mathbb{C}), GL_n(\mathbb{C})] = SL_n(\mathbb{C})$.

**(iii)**
Suppose $n\geq{3}$ and $n\neq 4$. For any $\sigma, \tau \in S_n$,  
  $$
  \mathrm{sgn}([\sigma, \tau]) = \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)\mathrm{sgn}(\tau)\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)^{-1}\mathrm{sgn}(\tau)^{-1} = 1\Rightarrow[\sigma,\tau] \in A_n ,
  $$
so $[S_n, S_n] \subseteq A_n$.

For $G=S_n$,
- If $n=1,2$, $S_n$ is abelian, so $[S_n,S_n]=\{1\}$.
- If $n=3$, $[S_3,S_3]=A_3\cong C_3$.
- If $n\ge5$, $[S_n,S_n]=A_n$ (since $A_n$ is simple and non‑abelian, and $S_n/A_n\cong C_2$ is abelian).
- The case $n=4$: $[S_4,S_4]=A_4$ (one can check that all 3‑cycles are commutators, and they generate $A_4$).

Therefore, $[S_n, S_n] = A_n$.
